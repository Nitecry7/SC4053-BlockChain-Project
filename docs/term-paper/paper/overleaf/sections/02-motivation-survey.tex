\subsection{Why Security over Privacy and Scalability}\label{sec:motivation}
On privacy, every order, limit and stop trigger is public, and public Ethereum activity lets an observer link one user's addresses: combining two embeddings of an address's position in the transaction graph shrank a user's anonymity set about threefold~\cite{src25beres2021}. % [25] p. 7 Sec. 6 (F4: Diff2Vec + Role2Vec, 1.6 bits, 3.03x); EM S8 (corrected); IOrderBook.sol:49, 52
That study links accounts rather than trading strategies, and a privacy leak alone exposes intent without moving funds. % [25] caveat (EM S8)
On scalability, an on-chain book is expensive: in its worst case, with every order marketable, the call market Lissy (which matches all orders of a period at once) could close only 38 to 152 trades in one block under the July 2020 gas limit, depending on its data structure~\cite{src09moosavi2023}. % [09] p. 6 to 7 Table 5 (F3), p. 6 worst case, p. 4 gas limit 11,741,495 EthStats July 2020; EM M5e (corrected)
On an Arbitrum testnet, moving that market to a rollup, a layer-two chain that posts its data to Ethereum, cut the layer-one gas of a market close by 99.88\%, not counting layer-two work~\cite{src09moosavi2023}. % [09] p. 7 Table 6, p. 8 (F6); EM S3
Yet rollups without a public mempool, the pool of pending transactions, still carry extractable value, the profit from choosing transaction order: mainly arbitrage and liquidations, with no sandwiches detected, although cross-layer sandwiches remain feasible~\cite{src23torres2024}. % [23] p. 1 to 2, p. 4 Sec. 2.3, p. 7 Sec. 4 (F2 to F4, F6, F9); EM S4, M11f
Security comes first because, in this engine, weaknesses in the other two concerns surface as lost value: three of our four attacks run through loop bounds we introduced to save gas, and we argue that the public book and stop list tell the adversary where to aim. % OB:20-21; EM S1; IOrderBook.sol:49, 52 (S6, design inference, hedged)
Qin et al. argue that extractable value is likely to remain while execution is transparent and one party can choose the order~\cite{src05qin2022}. % [05] p. 13 Sec. VII (F4, corrected); EM S5 (corrected)

\subsection{Related Work}\label{sec:related}
\emph{Front-running and extractable value.}
Eskandari et al. classify front-running as displacement, insertion or suppression and name fully on-chain order books as open to the first two~\cite{src02eskandari2019}; their analysis is qualitative and was written while such books were still being built by several projects. % [02] p. 4 to 5 Sec. 3, p. 7 Sec. 4.1 (F1, F3)
Daian et al. measured bots bidding up gas prices for transaction order in priority gas auctions on proof-of-work Ethereum, before today's builder markets, showing that transaction order could be bought there~\cite{src01daian2020}. % [01] p. 1, p. 5 to 6 Sec. III-C (F1); EM C1e caveat
Zhou et al. present their study as the first formal and quantitative treatment of sandwich attacks, and they explicitly exclude limit order books~\cite{src04zhou2021}. % [04] p. 1 Sec. I (F2); paraphrased, not quoted
Qin et al. found 750{,}529 sandwich attacks on Uniswap, Sushiswap and Bancor, which earned the attackers 174.34M USD in all~\cite{src05qin2022}. % [05] p. 4 Sec. IV-A2 (F2); EM C1d
These are all AMM results, and the mechanism differs from ours. % EM C1d caveat
On an AMM the price of a trade follows deterministically from the pool's reserves, so a sandwich moves the reserves before the victim and restores them after~\cite{src04zhou2021}; in a limit order book the price is set by resting orders that the book must store and match~\cite{src21ferreira2023}, so we argue an attacker there has to trade against the victim's order or change which side is the maker. % [04] p. 1 (F2), p. 4 Sec. III (F10), p. 3 to 5 (F1); [21] p. 2 to 3 Sec. 1 (F10)
The AMM numbers show that ordering attacks pay in practice, not how large they are on an order book held in a contract. % EM C1d caveat

\emph{Economic security.}
Babel et al. define extractable value as a formal measure of the economic security of composed contracts and show that composing contracts can raise it~\cite{src08babel2023}. % [08] p. 5 Sec. 1 (F1), p. 16 Sec. 4 (F5, F6); EM S9
We argue their insight applies by analogy inside one contract: our engine's rules each look safe in isolation, and the attacks come from combining them. % EM S1; reasoning
Zhou et al. count 28 on-chain oracle manipulation incidents (15\%), the most frequent protocol-layer type in decentralized finance on Ethereum and BNB Smart Chain from April 2018 to April 2022, and few academic papers on price oracle attacks~\cite{src06zhou2023}; our stop trigger is such a price. % [06] p. 1 Abstract, p. 12 Insight 3 (F3, F4); EM S10, M6

\emph{Order books on chain and batch auctions.}
Lissy measures what an on-chain book costs in gas and argues that a call market resists basic insertion and displacement because time priority disappears inside a call~\cite{src09moosavi2023}; we consider it the closest published system to ours. % [09] p. 4 to 7 (F2, F3), p. 8 Sec. 5 Table 7 (F7); EM M5b
Our book differs because it is continuous: each order is matched on arrival at the resting order's price, with earlier orders first among equal prices, so the order in which transactions land decides who trades with whom and who is the maker, which the fill-cap sandwich and stop snipe of Sec.~\ref{sec:analysis} exploit. % OB:38-40, 67-68, 115, 276; EM C1a, C3a
In Lissy, shuffling the orders within a call gives the same outcome, so basic insertion and displacement gain nothing; suppression stays possible but costly, a hybrid attack on price improvement is defused by paying that improvement to the miner, and an exact price tie is broken by time, a case its authors call improbable but where front-running remains possible~\cite{src09moosavi2023}. % [09] p. 8 to 10 Sec. 5 (F7), p. 9 to 10 hybrid attack, p. 11 Sec. 6.1 (F8)
Lissy also drops cancellation, because cancels can be displaced until after the call closes and removable orders need a heavier data structure~\cite{src09moosavi2023}; on a continuous book a visible cancel can be front-run by a fill~\cite{src02eskandari2019}, and our engine keeps cancellation. % [09] p. 11 Sec. 6.2 (F8); [02] p. 6 Sec. 4.1 (F4); OB:89-99
It pays for this in throughput: in the worst case one market close takes a whole block~\cite{src09moosavi2023}. % [09] p. 6 Sec. 3.3 ("monopolizing an entire Ethereum block"), worst case (F4)
Budish et al. argue that the continuous limit order book itself creates the speed race and propose frequent batch auctions, in which a speed advantage is worth only a fraction of its continuous value~\cite{src11budish2015}; their evidence comes from traditional futures and equity markets, not a blockchain. % [11] Sec. 1, Sec. 7.2 Prop. 8 (F1, F6), 2013 draft, no pages; EM M5a

\emph{Defences against reordering.}
Heimbach and Wattenhofer compare seven categories of defence (their Secs. 5.1 to 5.7) on seven measures: decentralization, security, scope, jostling (competition between traders for inclusion at a preferential position in the block), goodput (useful throughput), delay and cost; none meets every requirement~\cite{src18heimbach2022}. % [18] p. 5 Sec. 4 (F2, F11), pp. 5 to 11 Sec. 5 (F3), p. 1, p. 11 Sec. 6 (F5); EM M11a
In their qualitative scoring, on-chain commit and reveal does well on scope, jostling and decentralization but poorly on cost, because every order needs two on-chain transactions, and on delay, because the reveal comes some blocks later; an attacker may also simply not reveal~\cite{src18heimbach2022}. % [18] p. 10 to 11 Sec. 5.6 (F6); EM M4c
Canidio and Danos show that it deters the most damaging front-running for one extra message and a one-block delay, but only when guessing the hidden order is hard (their Prop. 2), and their model excludes contracts that resolve competing claims, such as auctions~\cite{src20canidio2024}. % [20] p. 16 Sec. 3.1 Prop. 2 (F5, F6), p. 27 to 28 Sec. 6 (F10); EM M4a
In a price-time book where the number of competing orders is predictable, an attacker can commit several orders and reveal only the profitable ones~\cite{src02eskandari2019}. % [02] p. 15 Sec. 6.2 (F8); EM M4b
Every deterministic sequencing rule leaves some instances in which an AMM block producer gains risk-free profit~\cite{src21ferreira2023}, and Choudhuri et al. measure the decryption latency of their own encrypted-mempool scheme and report a flaw, since fixed, in a different, deployed one~\cite{src29choudhuri2024}. % [21] p. 4, p. 17 Thm 4.2, p. 25 Sec. 6 (F4, F5); [29] p. 6 to 7 Sec. 3 (F1, F3), p. 12 Table 2 (F5); EM M11c, M11d (corrected)
Every one of these defences leaves the matching rule as it is, so we argue that none of them, used alone, can stop a weakness that the attacker sets off within a single transaction. % reasoning from EV E9 atomic

\subsection{The Gap}\label{sec:gap}
We did not find a measurement of front-running on a smart-contract limit order book, nor a study of stop hunting through reference-price manipulation in crypto markets. % notes/PHASE4_GAPS.md gaps a, b
The deployed order-book DEXs whose trading was measured in the work we read kept their books off-chain~\cite{src12victor2021}, and the measured sandwiches were on AMMs~\cite{src04zhou2021,src05qin2022}. % [12] p. 2 Sec. 2.2.1 (F1); [04] p. 1 (F2); [05] p. 4 (F2); EM C1d
Our analysis of the attacks therefore rests on our own tests, with the literature as analogy. % Sec. III; EM C1d caveat
