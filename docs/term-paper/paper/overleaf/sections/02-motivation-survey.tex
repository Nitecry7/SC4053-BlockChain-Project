\subsection{Why Security over Privacy and Scalability}\label{sec:motivation}
Privacy matters for our DEX because every order, limit and stop trigger is public, and public Ethereum activity can be linked back to users: three behavioural features together shrink a user's anonymity set about threefold~\cite{src25beres2021}. % [25] p. 7 Sec. 6 (F4); EM S8; IOrderBook.sol:49, 52
That study profiles accounts rather than trading strategies, and a privacy leak alone exposes intent without moving funds. % [25] caveat (EM S8)
Scalability matters because an on-chain book is expensive: the call market Lissy could close at most 38 to 152 trades per Ethereum block under the 2021 gas limit, depending on its data structure~\cite{src09moosavi2023}. % [09] p. 6 to 7 Table 5 (F3); EM S2, M5e
A rollup cuts the layer-one gas sharply, but rollups without a public mempool still carry extractable value, mainly arbitrage rather than sandwiches~\cite{src09moosavi2023,src23torres2024}. % [09] p. 7 Table 6 (F6); [23] p. 2, p. 4 Sec. 2.3, p. 7 Sec. 4 (F2 to F4, F6); EM S3, S4, M11f
We rank security first because in our engine the other two concerns turn into security losses.
The fixed loop bounds, which we added for scalability, are the mechanism of three of our four attacks, and the public book and stop list are what let the adversary aim them. % OB:20-21; EM S1; IOrderBook.sol:49, 52 (S6, design inference)
Qin et al. trace extractable value to the same pair of causes, transparent execution and ordering that one party can choose~\cite{src05qin2022}. % [05] p. 13 Sec. VII (F4); EM S5

\subsection{Related Work}\label{sec:related}
\emph{Front-running and extractable value.}
Eskandari et al. classify front-running as displacement, insertion or suppression and name fully on-chain order books as open to the first two~\cite{src02eskandari2019}; their analysis is qualitative and predates working on-chain books. % [02] p. 4 to 5 Sec. 3, p. 7 Sec. 4.1 (F1, F3); note sec. 4 limits
Daian et al. measured bots bidding up gas prices for transaction order in priority gas auctions~\cite{src01daian2020}, which shows that ordering can be bought, although on proof-of-work Ethereum before today's builder markets. % [01] p. 1, p. 5 to 6 Sec. III-C (F1); EM C1e caveat
Zhou et al. describe their work as the first to formalize and quantify sandwich attacks, and they explicitly leave limit order books out of scope~\cite{src04zhou2021}. % [04] p. 1 Sec. I (F2); checklist wording
Qin et al. found 750{,}529 sandwich attacks on Ethereum worth 174.34M USD~\cite{src05qin2022}. % [05] p. 4 Sec. IV-A2 (F2); EM C1d
These measurements are all on AMMs, where an attacker moves a pool price; they show that ordering attacks pay at scale, not how large they are on a contract order book. % EM C1d caveat

\emph{Economic security.}
Babel et al. define extractable value as a formal measure of the economic security of composed contracts and show that composing contracts can raise it~\cite{src08babel2023}. % [08] p. 5 Sec. 1 (F1), p. 16 Sec. 4 (F5, F6); EM S9
We argue their insight transfers directly: each of our engine's rules is safe alone, and the attacks come from combining them. % EM S1; reasoning
Zhou et al. find on-chain price manipulation in 15\% of DeFi incidents on Ethereum and BSC from 2018 to 2022 and note that few academic papers address it~\cite{src06zhou2023}; our stop trigger is such a price, set inside the engine. % [06] p. 1 Abstract, p. 12 Insight 3 (F3, F4); EM S10

\emph{Order books on chain and batch auctions.}
Lissy measures what an on-chain book costs in gas and argues that a call market, which matches all orders of a period at once, resists insertion and displacement because time priority disappears inside a call~\cite{src09moosavi2023}; it is the closest prior design to ours, but it is a call market, not a continuous book. % [09] p. 4 to 7 (F2, F3), p. 8 to 10 Sec. 5 Table 7 (F7); EM M5b
Budish et al. argue that the continuous limit order book itself creates the speed race and propose frequent batch auctions, in which a speed advantage is worth only a fraction of its continuous value~\cite{src11budish2015}; their evidence comes from traditional futures and equity markets, not a blockchain. % [11] Sec. 1, Sec. 7.2 Prop. 8 (F1, F6), 2013 draft, no pages; EM M5a

\emph{Defences against reordering.}
Commit and reveal hides an order until a later reveal and deters the most damaging front-running at the cost of an extra message and a delay~\cite{src20canidio2024}, but in a price-time book an attacker can commit several orders and reveal only the profitable ones~\cite{src02eskandari2019}. % [20] p. 16 Sec. 3.1 (F5, F6); [02] p. 15 Sec. 6.2 (F8); EM M4a, M4b
Heimbach and Wattenhofer score seven families of chain-level defences and find that none meets every requirement~\cite{src18heimbach2022}. % [18] p. 1, p. 11 Sec. 6 (F5); EM M11a
Verifiable sequencing rules limit what a block producer may do, yet no deterministic rule removes risk-free producer profit on an AMM~\cite{src21ferreira2023}, and encrypted mempools add measured decryption latency and have had implementation flaws~\cite{src29choudhuri2024}. % [21] p. 17 Thm 4.2, p. 25 Sec. 6 (F4, F5); [29] p. 6 Sec. 3, p. 12 Table 2 (F1, F5); EM M11c, M11d
None of these changes the matching rule itself, so we argue that none alone removes a weakness an attacker can trigger inside one transaction. % reasoning from EV E9 atomic

\subsection{The Gap}\label{sec:gap}
We did not find a measurement of front-running on a smart-contract limit order book, nor a study of stop hunting through reference-price manipulation in crypto markets. % notes/PHASE4_GAPS.md gaps a, b
The order books that were measured matched off-chain, and the measured sandwiches were on AMMs. % [12] p. 2 Sec. 2.2.1 (F1); [04] p. 1 (F2); EM C1d
Our analysis of C1 to C3 therefore rests on our own tests, with the literature as analogy. % Sec. III; EM C1d caveat
