## 2. Findings
| # | Finding (own words) | Pointer (p. N, Sec.) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Consistency and liveness say nothing about how the log is ordered; in leader-based protocols (most used in practice) a malicious leader can pick any order. | p. 1 (Abstract); p. 3 (Sec. 1) |
| 2 | Motivation cites Daian et al.: bots on Ethereum extracting "upwards of USD 6M" from unsophisticated users through ordering manipulation. | p. 3 (Sec. 1) |
| 3 | Receive-order-fairness ("if at least a gamma fraction of nodes receive tx before tx', output tx first") is impossible because of the Condorcet paradox: Thm 4.3 for gamma <= (n-1)/n when the external network is asynchronous or delta_ext >= n, even with no adversary; Thm 4.4 extends to gamma <= 1 once f >= 1. | p. 4 (Thm 1.2); pp. 21 to 22 (Sec. 4.1, Ex. 4.2, Thm 4.3, Thm 4.4) |
| 4 | The achievable relaxation is block-order-fairness: tx may not be delivered in a later block than tx'; paradoxical cycles are put in the same block, but consistency still fixes one total order. | pp. 4 to 5 (Def. 1.3); p. 24 (Def. 4.7) |
| 5 | Aequitas works in three stages: gossip via FIFO broadcast, agreement on whose local logs count (Set-BA), finalization on a dependency graph collapsed into strongly connected components. | pp. 5 to 6 (Sec. 1.1); pp. 24 to 26 (Sec. 5) |
| 6 | Four Aequitas variants; corruption bound n > 2f/(2gamma-1) in the synchronous setting and n > 4f/(2gamma-1) in any setting; all give only weak (or eventual weak) liveness. | p. 7 (Fig. 1) |
| 7 | Weak liveness: a transaction is only output once later transactions arrive to "flush out" earlier ones. | p. 6 (Sec. 1.1); pp. 15 to 16 (Sec. 2.3); p. 30 (Sec. 6.1) |
| 8 | In the leader-based variant the leader may still choose the order of transactions that are not required to be ordered (for example within one strongly connected component); proposals breaking fairness are rejected. | p. 6 (Sec. 1.1); p. 25 (Sec. 5); p. 41 (Sec. 8.1) |
| 9 | Encrypting transactions until ordering is fixed (censorship resistance) is strictly weaker than order-fairness: metadata leaks, blind reordering of ciphertexts, and a leader colluding with a user knows that user's ciphertext. | pp. 8 to 9 (Sec. 1.2) |
| 10 | Send-order-fairness would need trusted client timestamps and a synchronous external network; current TEEs cannot give trusted global time. Model is permissioned (n known nodes). | p. 10 (Sec. 2); p. 42 (Sec. 8.3) |

## 4. Limits for an on-chain order book
- Permissioned consensus with a known node set (p. 10, Sec. 2); Ethereum-style public chains and rollup sequencers are not modelled.
- Fairness is defined on receive order at consensus nodes. An attacker who observes a victim and then submits later is ordered after the victim (own inference from Def. 4.7), but C2 (stop hunt by a self-trade) and C4 (dust wall) do not depend on jumping ahead of a victim, so order-fairness does not address them (own inference; the paper does not discuss DEX attacks beyond the motivation on p. 3).
- C1 and C3 dust can be placed well before the victim arrives, which order-fairness allows (own inference).
- Requires changing the consensus protocol: not something the contract can deploy (M11 only).
- No measurements, costs only asymptotic (communication O(nB(s) + nB(1)) per transaction, p. 32, Sec. 6.1).

## 5. Claim check
- Summary given: "order-fairness as a third consensus property."
- Verdict: supported
- Evidence: p. 1 (Abstract) proposes "a third consensus property: transaction order-fairness" next to consistency and liveness; p. 4 (Sec. 1.1) lists it as a contribution. Precision note for the paper: the natural form (receive-order-fairness) is proven impossible (Thm 4.3, 4.4, pp. 21 to 22); what Aequitas achieves is the weaker block-order-fairness with weak liveness only (Fig. 1, p. 7), in a permissioned setting (p. 10).
