## 2. Findings
(page numbers = 2013 draft PDF pages; printed page in brackets)
| # | Finding (own words) | Pointer (p. N, Sec.) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Core thesis: the continuous limit order book (CLOB) is "a flawed market design"; exchanges should use frequent batch auctions, i.e. "uniform-price sealed-bid double auctions conducted at frequent but discrete time intervals, e.g., every 1 second". | p. 1 (Abstract), p. 2 [1] (Sec. 1), p. 57 [56] (Sec. 9) |
| 2 | The CLOB uses price-time priority and processes order submissions and withdrawals "in serial, that is, one-at-a-time in order of their receipt"; this serial processing drives the theory. Stop-loss and other order types are described as proxy instructions for generating limit orders. | p. 9 to 10 [8 to 9] (Sec. 2) |
| 3 | Empirics (ES vs SPY, direct-feed CME and NYSE data 2005 to 2011): median return correlation 0.0923 at 10 ms and 0.0073 at 1 ms (2011); about a thousand arbitrage opportunities per day worth "on the order of $75 million per year"; Table 3 mean 891 arbs/day; median duration fell from 97 ms (2005) to 7 ms (2011) while median profit stayed about 0.08 index points, so the prize is a "constant" of the design, not competed away. | p. 3 [2], p. 5 [4] (Sec. 1), p. 21 [20] (Table 3) |
| 4 | Mechanism: when public information moves, the liquidity provider races many snipers to update a now-stale quote; since only the first message counts, the provider usually loses; sniping cost is priced into wider spreads and thinner books, paid by fundamental investors. | p. 5 to 6 [4 to 5] (Sec. 1) |
| 5 | Speed investment is a prisoner's dilemma: welfare would be higher by N*·c_speed if market makers could commit not to invest, yet each has a dominant strategy to invest (Proposition 4); the arms race is "an inevitable consequence" of the CLOB design. | p. 6 [5] (Sec. 1), p. 38 [37] (Prop. 4) |
| 6 | Batching fixes this in two ways: a δ speed advantage is worth only δ/τ of its continuous-market value (1 ms vs a 1 s batch = 1/1000); and simultaneous arrivals compete on price, not speed, so stale quotes are bid up to the correct value (Proposition 8: in the CLOB a slow provider is vulnerable for proportion 1 of jumps, in the batch auction δ/τ). | p. 6 to 7 [5 to 6] (Sec. 1), p. 45 to 47 [44 to 46] (Sec. 7.2, Prop. 8) |
| 7 | FBA definition: batch length τ; limit and market orders accepted and withdrawable during the interval; orders not visible during the interval ("sealed bid"), which is "important to prevent gaming"; aggregate supply and demand published after; single uniform clearing price; ties at the clearing price rationed, suggested rule: earlier batch intervals first, then pro rata within the last interval, so "time priority is only relevant across batch intervals". | p. 42 to 43 [41 to 42] (Sec. 7.1, fn. 33, fn. 35) |
| 8 | Stability arguments (informal): batch clearing is O(n log n); 250,000 orders clear in about 10 ms in a C++ test; discrete time removes processing backlog and gives a clean audit trail. Optimal batch interval is left open. | p. 55 to 57 [54 to 56] (Sec. 8, fn. 41; Sec. 9) |

## 4. Limits for an on-chain order book
- TradFi venue (CME, NYSE) with millisecond latency; no blockchain, no gas, no block producer who can reorder or insert; the attacker in their model is only faster, not able to see and reorder pending orders.
- "Sealed bid" assumes a trusted exchange hides orders; on a public chain this needs commit and reveal or encryption (not discussed).
- Uniform-price clearing of many orders on-chain hits the gas limits measured in source 09 (about 38 to 152 trades per block).
- No stop orders or stop-trigger manipulation discussed.
- Draft version: numbers may differ from the QJE article.

- Summary given: "the continuous limit order book rewards speed by design; proposes uniform-price frequent batch auctions."
- Verdict: supported
- Evidence: "rewards speed by design": p. 1 (Abstract) says the correlation breakdown creates "technical arbitrage opportunities, available to whomever is fastest", the arms race is "a prisoner's dilemma built directly into the market design" (p. 1), and "an inevitable consequence of the continuous limit order book market design" (p. 6 [5]); Prop. 4 at p. 38 [37].
