## 2. Findings
| # | Finding (own words) | Pointer (p. N, Sec.) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Three gas-focused vulnerability classes: unbounded mass operations (Sec. 3.1), non-isolated external calls / "wallet griefing" (Sec. 3.2), integer overflows of loop counters (Sec. 3.3). The term "DoS with block gas limit" is NOT used as a class name; Sec. 3.1 says unbounded loops commonly lead to denial of service for all transactions that must run the loop. | pp. 5 to 8 (116:5 to 116:8, Sec. 3) |
| 2 | Root cause: contracts may reach a state (e.g. a storage data structure grows a lot) where no transaction can supply enough gas to run their code, because the block gas limit cannot be surpassed. | p. 6 (116:6, Sec. 3) |
| 3 | Unbounded mass operation: loops whose iteration count is set by user-controlled data can exceed the block gas limit; every transaction that must run the loop is denied service. Fix: favor pull over push, or a resumable loop that checks gas and saves its position. | p. 6 (116:6, Sec. 3.1) |
| 4 | Resumable gas-checked loops are rarely used correctly in deployed contracts (authors' finding). | p. 7 (116:7, Sec. 3.1); p. 22 (116:22, Sec. 6.3.2) |
| 5 | Wallet griefing: a push payment inside a loop that aborts on failure lets one recipient with a failing fallback lock the whole loop; resumability does not help because the failed send re-fails. | pp. 7 to 8 (116:7 to 116:8, Sec. 3.2); p. 19 (Sec. 5.3) |
| 6 | Attacker motives listed: lower ETH price, fame, destroying competitors' contracts, blackmail; attacker typically must spend funds to grow the data structure. | p. 8 (116:8, Sec. 3.4) |
| 7 | The vulnerability queries require a loop bound by an array whose size can be increased by a public function; the attacker must be able to append to the iterated array. | pp. 17 to 19 (Sec. 5.2, 5.3, Fig. 9, Fig. 10) |
| 8 | Results (blockchain as of 9 Apr 2018): flagged contracts hold 7.07 million ETH (about $2.8 billion at $400.72/ETH); 4.1% of contracts flagged for unbounded iteration, 0.12% wallet griefing, 1.2% loop overflow; precision 13/16 = 81% on 13 manually inspected contracts. | p. 20 (116:20, Sec. 6.1) |
| 9 | About half of contracts have dynamically bound loops, mostly iterating over arrays that represent accounts, bids, rounds, which can sometimes be grown just by calling public functions. | p. 15 (Sec. 5.2); p. 22 (Sec. 6.3.2) |
| 10 | Attack cost caveat: adding an element to an array costs about an order of magnitude more gas than iterating over one more element, so exploitation is not necessarily cheap. | p. 23 (116:23, Sec. 6.4) |

## 4. Limits for an on-chain order book
- Targets unbounded loops; our loops are bounded, so our risk is a different one (insertion reverts and fill caps), which MadMax does not study.
- Measured on Ethereum L1 mainnet as of 9 Apr 2018 (PoW, 2018 gas costs and block gas limit).
- Wallet griefing is about Ether send/transfer with a 2,300 gas stipend; our payouts are ERC20 transfers via SafeERC20, where the failure comes from the token contract, not a fallback.
- Static-analysis tool paper; findings are warnings with 81% precision on a small source-available sample (p. 20, p. 23 bias discussion).
- Contract counts are inconsistent in the paper: "6.3 million" (p. 3), "6.33 million contract instances ... 91.8k unique programs" (p. 20), "6.6 million" (p. 25). Use p. 20 if needed.

## 5. Claim check
- Summary given: "classifies gas-focused vulnerabilities such as unbounded loops."
- Verdict: supported
- Evidence: Sec. 3 (pp. 5 to 8) identifies some of the most common patterns of gas-focused vulnerabilities: unbounded mass operations (loops over user-controlled data), wallet griefing, and integer overflows. Contributions list includes the identification of gas-focused vulnerabilities (p. 3). Note the paper speaks of some of the most common patterns, so it is an identification of patterns, not a claimed complete taxonomy. Unbounded loops correspond to the paper's unbounded mass operations (also called unbounded iteration, p. 20).
